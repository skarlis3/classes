FYW Skeleton Proposal — ENGL 1181

This site is a working draft of a standardized skeleton for first-year writing classes at Macomb. The full proposal is for all FYW classes; the assignments, readings, and schedules gathered here are the ENGL 1181 versions being built out as the first models.

There are three tracks below. Each is a complete set of assignments, readings, and schedules — an instructor picks one and teaches it. Start with a track's Skeleton Draft for the overview, then read the assignment pages for detail.

The Proposal

Each FYW class gets a skeleton of core assignments and core readings.

Who uses it

  • Required for instructors (adjunct and PT) for all class modes.
  • Present as options for full-time faculty (who can either use them, not use them, or remix them).

Instructor choice built in

  • More than one set of assignments to choose from.
  • Smaller scaffolding assignments are provided as support, but can be modified or replaced at instructor discretion.
  • Eventually may create a form for getting approval for replacement assignments that still fit the outcomes and objectives.
  • The 8-week online master shell will eventually have things like discussion boards and/or hypothes.is (social annotation) assignments added; for now this is the central skeleton.

Rationale

  • To ensure that outcomes and objectives are being met for the class.
  • To have one central source of truth for the classes (instead of multiple master shells).
  • Can be used as the base for online shells.
  • Will give instructors a starting place for non-online classes or class lengths where there isn't a master shell.

How CORE and FLEX work

All three tracks use the same system:

  • CORE — required assignments and readings. Cannot be changed without departmental approval.
  • FLEX — scaffolding and recommended content. Can be altered, replaced, or supplemented freely, as long as the core projects and objectives remain supported.

Track A — Genres in Your Field

Students analyze a written genre from a professional field they're interested in — police reports, patient chart notes, lesson plans, client proposals. They gather real samples, identify the conventions that recur across them, and explain how the genre works in professional practice. A multimodal project follows, applying the same rhetorical and genre concepts in a new form.

Two projects plus a final reflection, scaffolded by ten writing activities. Offered in two pacings: a portfolio model, where students bundle a final revision with the reflection at the end of the semester, and a no-portfolio model, where the revised draft is the graded version.

  • Skeleton Draft — required and recommended assignments and readings
  • Units — each project with the readings and activities that feed it
  • Project 1: Genre Analysis Essay and Project 2: Multimodal Project
  • Writing Activities — WA1–10 plus Reflection & Checklist and GenAI & Genre
  • In-class and/or Lecture Topics — suggested topics for active learning, lectures, and discussion
  • Final Reflection
  • 16-Week Schedule — Portfolio and 16-Week Schedule — No Portfolio
  • Grading — three methods and two weight tables
  • Objectives Map — where each outcome and objective is covered

Track B — Digital Lives

This track focuses on one question, which the whole semester answers: how are our digital lives affecting our real lives? Students write a personal essay putting their own experience in conversation with the readings, then a rhetorical analysis of one piece of media pulled from their own feed, then an argument drawing on the course readings.

Three projects plus a final reflection, scaffolded by nine writing activities. Every project runs the same cycle: read, activity, rough draft, peer reading, final draft.

Adapted with permission from a colleague's course.

  • Skeleton Draft — required and recommended assignments and readings
  • Project 1: The Conversation Essay, Project 2: The Digital Rhetoric Essay, and Project 3: The Argument Essay
  • Writing Activities — WA1–9 plus Introductions and Peer Reading
  • Final Reflection
  • Readings — what each reading slot needs to do, and what currently fills it
  • 16-Week Schedule

Track C — Rhetoric & AI Ethics

Draft

Track C is newer than the other two and still has open items. The track name is a working title, the topic-strand articles need citations and access checks, and this track has no separate Final Reflection — reflection is distributed across the three projects instead. See the Skeleton Draft and Readings pages.

This track focuses on generative AI — as a subject students write about in the first half, and as a tool they use with disclosure in the second. Students choose one topic strand — the environment, misinformation, education, discrimination, labor, art and creativity, mental health, companionship — and keep it for the semester.

Three projects: a collaborative narrated presentation teaching the class about the group's issue, a rhetorical analysis essay of a single article about AI and education, and a PSA poster aimed at a student audience. Scaffolded by eleven writing activities plus two introduction activities, on a weekly rhythm of reading check, discussion, and short write.

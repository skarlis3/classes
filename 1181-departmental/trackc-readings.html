Reading Roles — Track C

Note to instructors

This track defines readings by the job each one does, so you can swap texts without touching any assignment. Each slot below says what the reading needs to do and what currently fills it. Assignments refer to readings by role — “your group’s article,” “the analysis article” — never by title.

The topic-strand articles

Supports Project 1, WA3, WA4, and supplies logos for Project 3.

  • Offer six to eight strands, each with one article. Students pick a strand; the choice forms their group and follows them all semester.
  • Each article should take a position on one ethical or social issue raised by generative AI, be readable in a sitting, and have an identifiable author with findable credentials — WA3 asks students to research the author’s authority and the publication’s audience, so anonymous or corporate-authored pieces don’t work.
  • Strands should be different enough that students are choosing a topic they care about, not a difficulty level.
  • Currently eight strands: AI and the Environment · AI and Misinformation · AI in Education · AI and Discrimination · AI and Labor · AI, Art, and Human Creativity · AI and Mental Health · AI Companionship.

Four of these could come from the textbook. Chapter 7 ends with four short news articles from The Conversation, all republishable and already in the book:

  • 7.5 News Article: Data Center Fights — fits the environment strand
  • 7.6 News Article: People Are Getting Their News From AI — fits misinformation
  • 7.7 News Article: Who Owns an AI Generated Song? — fits art and creativity
  • 7.8 News Article: Taylor Swift Trademarking Her Voice — fits art and creativity, or a strand on likeness and consent

The GenAI foundations reading

Supports the introductions activity and WA1, and frames the whole first unit.

  • A general introduction to what generative AI is and how it works, written for students rather than practitioners, durable enough not to date within a year.
  • 7.1 Understanding Generative AI fills this slot directly. The original course uses a chapter from a commercial textbook; 7.1 covers the same ground and is already in the book.

The analysis article

Supports Project 2 and WA5–WA7.

  • One article for the whole class. This is the text every student writes their rhetorical analysis of, so a shared article makes the claim discussion and the peer review comparable.
  • It needs all three appeals working visibly, a clear authorial persona, and a stance students can genuinely disagree about — the assignment asks them to judge whether it persuades, which only works if the answer isn’t obvious.
  • On the shorter side. Students read it many times.
  • Currently: John McWhorter, “My Students Are Using AI. So What?” (The Atlantic).

PSA support materials

Supports Project 3.

  • A guide to what a PSA is and does
  • A set of example PSAs students can look at
  • A how-to for whatever design tool you point them at
  • Two or three student sample PSAs from previous terms, if you have them

Access Notes

Instructors on this track are required to use the departmental OER, so it’s worth knowing how each non-textbook reading reaches students.

In the textbook — nothing extra required: 2.3, 2.4, 3.1, 3.2, 3.6, 4.1–4.5, 6.1, 6.2, 6.3, and all of Chapter 7.

Needs sourcing or checking:

  • The eight topic-strand articles are uploaded PDFs in the original shell, with no source recorded. Two are identifiable and both are paywalled at source: Ted Chiang’s “Why A.I. Isn’t Going to Make Art” (The New Yorker) and the McWhorter piece (The Atlantic). Before this goes to instructors, each strand article needs a citation and a check on whether students can actually reach it.
  • The analysis article has the same problem — The Atlantic is paywalled, and the original distributes a PDF.
  • The GenAI foundations reading in the original is a chapter from a commercial textbook. 7.1 replaces it at no cost.

The opportunity here is unusually good. Four of the eight strands can be filled from Chapter 7 today, and the foundations reading can be swapped for 7.1. That would leave only the analysis article and four strand articles needing open replacements — and since The Conversation is CC BY-ND and republishable, more strands could be built the same way 7.5–7.8 were.

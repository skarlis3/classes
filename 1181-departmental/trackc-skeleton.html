About This Track

Track C treats generative AI as the subject students write about and, in the second half, as a tool they learn to use transparently. Students pick one topic strand out of several — AI and the environment, misinformation, education, discrimination, labor, art and creativity, mental health, companionship — and that choice carries them through the whole semester.

Three projects in three different modes. Students first teach the class about their issue in a collaborative narrated presentation, then write a rhetorical analysis of a single article about AI and education, then design a PSA poster persuading a student audience about responsible GenAI use. Each project asks for the same rhetorical thinking in a different form: spoken and visual, written and analytical, then purely visual.

The weekly rhythm is fixed and students learn it in the first week: read, then discuss, then write a short reflective piece. The short writes are never busywork — each one produces material the next project needs.

GenAI appears in both roles. The first half studies writing about AI; the second half turns to writing with it, including a required exercise using a GenAI tool for feedback on a draft, a discussion of when disclosure is necessary, and an MLA citation assignment for AI use.

Sources are closed throughout: students use only assigned course readings, with no outside research at any point.

Required Major Assignments

  • Project 1: Group Presentation on an Issue in AI Ethics — collaborative, narrated, posted for the class to respond to
  • Project 2: Rhetorical Analysis Essay — 1,000–1,500 words analyzing the appeals in one article, ending in an evaluative claim
  • Project 3: PSA Poster — single-image visual persuasion about responsible GenAI use
  • Peer Review (a structured peer reading of the Project 2 draft, using a named protocol and a form)
  • Reflection (attached to each project rather than as a separate end-of-semester paper)

Optional but recommended

There is no required number of Writing Activities — use all of them, some of them, or none at all. Unlike some flex assignments, these can be dropped entirely, not just replaced.

They’re recommended because they break the major projects into steps, and each one produces something students reuse, so no project starts from a blank page. If you drop them, plan on scaffolding that work another way — the objectives listed under Required Minor Assignments above still need to be covered.

How the Pieces Depend on Each Other

Read this before swapping anything. This track has interlocks that aren’t obvious from any single assignment.

  • The topic strand a student picks early governs all three projects. It determines their reading, their presentation group, the article behind their short write, and the source of the logical claim in their PSA. Changing the strand list changes the whole semester for that student.
  • Project 1 is collaborative and group-assigned. Groups form when students choose strands. If you cut the strand choice, you have to replace the grouping mechanism too.
  • WA5 (evaluative claim) becomes Project 2’s thesis. The one-sentence claim from the discussion is refined in the short write and then argued across the essay.
  • The GenAI feedback exercise produces something later assignments require. The chat transcript from WA9 is what students cite in WA10; cutting one breaks the other.
  • Project 3’s logos must come from a previously assigned article. No new research. If students haven’t kept their Project 1 or 2 readings, the PSA has nothing to stand on.
  • Visual rhetoric is taught twice — once before the presentation and again before the PSA. Both projects are graded partly on design.

Structural Notes

  • Sources are closed. Students use only assigned course readings — no outside research at any point. This is 1181, not 1190.
  • Every week runs the same three beats: a reading check, a discussion, and a short write. Students learn the rhythm once and it never changes.
  • Reflection is distributed rather than saved for the end. Each project closes with its own reflective short write instead of a single final reflection paper. If your section needs a capstone reflection, WA11 is the natural place to build one.
  • GenAI is taught as both subject and tool, and the tool half is where this track does work the other two don’t.
  • No portfolio model. Each project is graded when due.

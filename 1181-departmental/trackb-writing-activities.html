Writing Activities (Flex)

Note to instructors

These scaffold toward the three projects. You may modify, replace, or add as needed. Each one carries a short note on what it's doing and what depends on it — check those before cutting anything. Nothing here names a specific reading; see the Readings page for what fills each slot.

The original course ran these as online discussions with post-by-Thursday, reply-by-Sunday rhythms. They're written here as activities, so they work as written assignments, in-class work, or discussions depending on your section.

WA1: Reading Annotation — Digital Habits

Feeds Project 1 and Project 3. The reading a student annotates here is the one they use in both.

  • Choose one of the argument readings and annotate it closely.
  • Address all of the following:
    • Highlight in blue the sentence or passage that best expresses the author's main argument. Comment on it, explaining in your own words what issue the author is writing about and what they're arguing.
    • Highlight in green at least three sentences that support that argument. Comment on each, explaining how it backs up the thesis.
    • Highlight at least three other points that interested you, in any color, and comment on why.
    • Mark one passage you found confusing, difficult, or wrong, and explain what made it hard.
    • At the end, add a closing note giving your opinion of the author's argument and of the topic. Where do you agree and disagree? What do you think matters here?

WA2: Your Online Habits

Generates raw material for Project 1. Students who write this well have half their essay's opening already.

  • Explain how your online habits shape your daily routines, your relationships, or your sense of yourself. Where do you see benefits, and where do you see tension?
  • Imagine you were talking to the author you annotated. Pick one specific point they make and say what you'd tell them about it.
  • Look at the data reading. How much does it match your own habits and experience?
  • ~200–250 words, in paragraphs

WA3: Process Freewrite

Feeds the Final Reflection. Deliberately unpolished — the point is to have something to reread at the end of the semester.

  • Write nonstop for about 10 minutes without worrying about correctness.
  • Reflect on how you wrote Project 1:
    • What steps did you take? What worked and what didn't?
    • What got hard, and how did you get past it?
    • What are you proudest of, and why?
  • Ungraded. Keep it.

WA4: Rhetorical Investigation

First hands-on rhetorical analysis, on a short text, before students take on a longer one alone in Project 2.

  • Take a short piece of digital content — a reel, a short video, a post. Assigning one text to the whole class makes discussion sharper; letting students choose gives you more range. Either works.
  • Rather than saying whether you liked it, analyze one of the following:
    • Logos — what's the actual claim? Is there reasoning here, or only feeling? What word choices support your reading?
    • Pathos — whose emotions are on display, and which ones is it trying to produce in you? Push past "it's funny." What specific choices create that?
    • Aesthetic — describe how it looks. What are the styling, setting, and framing doing for the message? Who is that look meant to attract?
    • Ethos — how does the creator build credibility? Does anything feel staged or manipulative? Are you persuaded?
  • Then connect your analysis to one of the digital identity readings.
  • ~200–250 words

WA5: Social Media Scrolling Log

Where Project 2's text comes from. Cut this and students arrive at Project 2 with nothing to analyze.

  • Spend about 10 minutes scrolling your usual app.
  • Scroll normally, not hunting for anything. Every third or fourth post, stop and record one entry. No particular kind of post is expected — the point is to see what's actually there.
  • Record five posts.
  • Below the log, write 150–200 words on which post was most persuasive and why, connecting to at least one of the digital identity readings.

Needs a log template — a simple grid where each row captures what the post was, who made it, what it seemed to want, and what caught your attention.

WA6: Reading Annotation — YEPS / NOPES / WOWS

Feeds Project 3. Same annotation habit as WA1, but the color system is the peer review vocabulary applied to published writing.

  • Choose a topic strand and annotate its required reading. Read the Project 3 assignment first — this is the reading your argument will be built on.
  • Address all of the following:
    • Highlight in blue the passage that best expresses the author's main argument, and explain it in your own words.
    • YEPS — highlight in green ideas that make sense, that you agree with, or that you could explain to someone else. Comment on each.
    • NOPES — highlight in pink ideas that confuse you, that you disagree with, or that you couldn't explain. Comment on each.
    • WOWS — highlight in yellow ideas that get you thinking, make you want to know more, or connect to your own life. Comment on each.
    • At the end, summarize what you understand the text to be saying and what you're taking from it.

WA7: Argument Outline

Project 3's prewriting. The assignment sheet sends students back to it.

  • Read the Project 3 assignment first.
  • Outline the argument you'll make. Include:
    • Introductory ideas — what conversation is happening around this topic?
    • A draft thesis. It can change.
    • Three supporting reasons or examples, each with a sentence or two on how it supports the thesis, and the author or title you'll use to back it up.
  • Write in full sentences with as much detail as you can manage.

WA8: Self-Assessment

Feeds the Final Reflection. Do it between the draft and the final version so it can still change the paper.

  • Reread the Project 3 assignment, then reread your own draft.
  • Write about how you did. You don't need to answer all of these — write something honest:
    • What were the assignment's goals, and how well did you meet them?
    • How well does your draft meet the criteria? Point to specifics.
    • What were your goals for this paper?
    • How did you actually write it? What worked?
    • What did you learn about your process that will help next time?
  • 250+ words

WA9: On Reflection

Prepares the Final Reflection. The first half is comprehension of the reflection reading; the second half is practice.

  • Read the reflection reading first.
  • First, summarize what reflection is and why it matters to learning. What's its value, and how should a student go about it?
  • Then, reflect on your own participation this semester. How did you approach the work? What were the strengths and weaknesses of what you contributed? What was your overall experience, and what would you do differently?
  • 250+ words

Unnumbered / Standalone

Introductions

First week, ungraded. Builds enough familiarity that peer review isn't cold three weeks later.

  • Choose any three prompts and answer them in ~150–250 words total:
    1. What digital platform or online space do you spend real time in, and why?
    2. What's something about your digital life most people wouldn't guess?
    3. What's a digital habit you like about yourself — or one you're trying to change?
    4. Has something online ever affected your offline life in a way that mattered? General reflections are fine.
    5. If your current digital life had a title — a movie, a playlist, a meme — what would it be?
    6. What do you hope this class helps you understand better about writing, technology, or yourself?
  • Then respond to at least two classmates: ask a real follow-up question, or say what you connected with. Agreement isn't the point; curiosity is.

Peer Reading CORE

Note to instructors

This is a core assignment, not a flex activity. It runs three times, identically, once before each project's final draft. The repetition is the point — students learn one feedback vocabulary and get better at it. YEPS / NOPES / WOWS is also the annotation system in WA6.

  • Post your own draft before reading anyone else's.
  • Read two classmates' drafts. If someone already has two readers, choose someone else.
  • Give feedback naming two instances of each:
    • YEPs — places you related to, ideas that made sense, ideas you agreed with, or choices you liked, and why
    • NOPEs — places that confused you, ideas you disagreed with, or choices that need work, and why
    • WOWs — places that got you thinking or excited, or choices that struck you as unusual or impressive
  • Then write briefly about what you learned from reading their paper. Compare their choices to your own.
  • Minimum 250 words per reading.

Tell students explicitly that a rough draft is allowed to be incomplete and imperfect — the original course says so in every peer review, and it changes what students are willing to post.

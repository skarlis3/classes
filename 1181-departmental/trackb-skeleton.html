ENGL 1181 — Track B: Digital Rhetoric & Social Media

DRAFT

Adapted with permission from a colleague's course. The original runs as an 8-week online section; this is the transferable version — no fixed dates, no LMS-specific instructions, and no modality assumed.

Course question, running through the whole semester: How are our digital lives affecting our real lives?

Every reading, activity, and project answers that question from a different angle. Students end the semester having written a personal essay, a rhetorical analysis, and an argument — all on the same question, moving further from their own experience each time.

Required Assignments & Readings (Core)

Core assignments and readings ensure that the course outcomes and objectives are met and also help provide continuity for students taking the first-year writing sequence at Macomb. These cannot be changed without departmental approval (see ENGL 1181 Assignment Change Request Form).

Required Major Assignments

  • Project 1: The Conversation Essay — the student's own experience in conversation with assigned readings
  • Project 2: The Digital Rhetoric Essay — rhetorical analysis of one piece of digital media the student chooses
  • Project 3: The Argument Essay — an argument answering the course question, supported by course readings
  • Peer Review (a structured peer reading of a draft before each project's final version)
  • Final Reflection (end of semester)

Required Readings

This track groups readings into four sets by the job each one does, not by title, so instructors can choose the specific texts. See the Readings page for what each slot needs and what currently fills it.

Set 1 — Digital habits and the self (supports Project 1)

  • A data reading: research or survey data on how people actually use digital media
  • An argument reading: a short essay taking a position on digital life and the self. Offer at least two options so students choose which to annotate.
  • Reading: 3.5 Academic Writing as a Genre
  • Reading: 10.4 Weaving Personal Experience into Academic Writing
  • At least one from Chapter 2 (reading strategies)
  • At least one from Chapter 4 (4.1–4.3, the writing process)

Set 2 — Digital identity and rhetoric (supports Project 2)

  • One or two digital identity readings: how identity is built and performed in networked spaces
  • Reading: 3.1 What is Rhetoric?
  • Reading: 3.2 What is the Rhetorical Situation?
  • Reading: 6.1 What are Multimodal Texts?
  • Reading: 6.2 Understanding Visual Rhetoric
  • Reading: 6.3 Visual Rhetoric and Digital Writing

Set 3 — Topic strands (supports Project 3)

Offer two or more strands so students choose a topic to argue about. Each strand needs one required reading students annotate closely and one reference reading they skim for specifics.

  • Reading: 3.6 Understanding Essays
  • Reading: 4.5 Revising & Orders of Concern
  • A GenAI reading from Chapter 7

Set 4 — Reflection (supports the Final Reflection)

  • A reflection reading: what reflection is and why it matters to learning

How the Pieces Depend on Each Other

Read this before swapping anything. This track has interlocks that aren't obvious from any single assignment.

  • The reading a student annotates in WA1 is the reading they use in Project 1 — and again in Project 3. Changing the Set 1 options changes all three.
  • WA5 (Scrolling Log) is where Project 2's text comes from. Students choose their analysis subject out of what they logged. Cut WA5 and students arrive at Project 2 with nothing to analyze.
  • WA7 (Argument Outline) is Project 3's prewriting. The assignment sheet tells students to use it.
  • WA3 (Process Freewrite) and WA8 (Self-Assessment) feed the Final Reflection. They're deliberately low-stakes; their value is what students can look back at in Week 16.
  • The annotation in WA6 uses the same YEPS / NOPES / WOWS frame as peer review. Students learn one feedback vocabulary and apply it to published writing and to each other.

Structural Notes

  • Sources are closed. Students use only assigned course readings — no outside research at any point. This is 1181, not 1190.
  • Every project runs the same cycle: read → activity → rough draft → peer reading → final draft → optional revision. Students learn the rhythm once.
  • Audience is a live choice on every project, and students are encouraged to switch. This is where much of the rhetorical work happens.
  • Optional revision after each project, requiring a written account of what changed and why. The original course grades these as extra credit so students who skip revision aren't penalized; that's an instructor call.
  • No portfolio model. Each project is graded when due.
  • This track is rhetoric-focused rather than genre-focused. Where Track A has 3.3 Navigating Genres in the core, this track has 3.1 What is Rhetoric?

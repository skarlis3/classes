Point Breakdown & Grading

Grading Methods

Instructors are not required to use a specific grading method. The following are approaches that work well with scaffolded, process-based writing courses. Instructors may use one of these or combine elements from multiple methods.

Completion-Based + Criteria-Based (Hybrid)

This method uses two different grading approaches depending on where an assignment falls in the writing process. Supporting assignments and drafts are graded on completion, while final projects are graded on quality.

For supporting assignments and drafts (rough draft, revised draft, writing activities): Use a check system to grade on completion, engagement, and meeting requirements — not on polish. The goal is to keep students moving through the process toward their polished drafts.

  • ✓+ (meets all requirements) / ✓ (missing one requirement) / ✓− (missing two requirements) / NC (not submitted or missing three or more requirements)

For final projects (Genre Analysis Final Draft, Multimodal Project, Final Reflection): Grade on quality along with requirements, using points-based rubrics or holistic grading (A/B/C/D/E scale) — instructor's choice.

This hybrid approach reduces grading burden on in-progress work while still holding students accountable for quality on their final products.

Contract Grading

In contract grading, grade levels are tied to how much work a student completes satisfactorily rather than averaging quality scores across assignments. The instructor and students agree on the terms at the start of the semester. Quality feedback still happens through comments and revision — but the grade itself is determined by completion and engagement, not by how "good" the writing is.

An example contract for this class might look like:

GradeRequirements
A Complete all WAs, all drafts (rough + revised + final), peer review, multimodal project, and final reflection. All work meets assignment requirements. No more than 2 late submissions.
B Complete all WAs, all drafts, peer review, multimodal project, and final reflection. All work meets assignment requirements. Up to 4 late submissions, or one assignment may be missing.
C Complete all core assignments (drafts, peer review, multimodal project, final reflection). At least 7 of the WAs completed. Work meets most requirements.
Below C Missing core assignments or significant gaps in WA completion.

Contract grading works well for scaffolded classes because the course design already assumes students improve through the process. The grade rewards doing the work, and the feedback loop handles quality.

Specifications Grading (Specs Grading)

In specs grading, each assignment has a clear set of specifications. Work is marked Satisfactory or Not Yet Satisfactory — no partial credit, no letter grades on individual assignments. Students who receive "Not Yet" can typically revise and resubmit (instructors set a revision policy, such as a limited number of revision attempts per semester).

For this class, specs might look like:

  • WAs: Each has a checklist of specs drawn from the assignment requirements (e.g., WA1: identified rhetorical situation of 2–3 texts, used signal phrases, 200+ words). Meet all specs = Satisfactory.
  • Rough Draft: Specs are the existing requirements — 1,300–1,700 words, addresses all required topics, evidence from 3+ samples, MLA citations. Meet them = Satisfactory.
  • Peer Review: Specs might include: provided feedback on all required areas, feedback is substantive (not just "looks good"), completed on time.
  • Final Draft / Multimodal Project: These could use tiered specs — a "Satisfactory" tier and an "Exemplary" tier — to differentiate A-level work from B-level work.

The final course grade is determined by how many assignments reach Satisfactory or Exemplary. This method pairs well with the existing assignment requirements, since the specs are largely already written into the assignments.

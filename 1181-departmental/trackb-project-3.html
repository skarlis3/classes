Project 3: The Argument Essay

Note to instructors

The cumulative project. Students argue a position on the course question and use course readings as evidence — no new research. The 80/20 framing below is doing real work: it's what keeps this from becoming a source-stitching exercise. WA7 is the prewriting, and the assignment sheet points students back to it.

Overview

You'll write an argument that answers the course question: How do our digital lives impact our lives in the "real world"? You'll support it with your own experience, insight, and observation, and use three to five course readings as evidence.

This is the cumulative project — it draws on everything you've read and written this semester.

Choosing Your Audience

Same three options as before — peers, family or elders, the college community.

Prompt

Make an argument about how our digital lives affect our lives offline. Most of what's in this paper will be yours:

  • Your thesis is your own opinion.
  • Your supporting reasons are your own ideas.
  • The explanations, examples, and reasoning behind them are your own.
  • The readings supply evidence that backs up ideas you already have.

The 80/20 rule: at least 80% of the paper should be your own ideas and words. Don't assemble the authors' arguments into a paper — they're there to support yours.

What Your Essay Needs to Do

  • Introduce the topic in your own words, with enough background for your audience. Narrow to a specific issue you actually care about, then state your thesis as an answer to the course question.
  • Build body paragraphs — as many as you need — giving reasons your thesis holds. Each one needs a topic sentence tied to a supporting reason, real development with your own examples and explanations, one main idea per paragraph, and transitions between them. Source material goes in to support your point, not to make it.
  • Take on a counterargument — an objection to your argument, not to the authors' — and answer it with your own reasoning.
  • Conclude by reaffirming your thesis and saying why it should matter to your reader. Answer "so what?" and look ahead.
  • Include a Works Cited page.

Using the Readings

Use three to five readings, and no more:

  • Required: the argument reading you annotated for Project 1
  • Required: the topic-strand reading you annotated in WA6
  • Optional: any other assigned course readings

No outside sources. No additional research is needed.

Because you're writing your own ideas alongside several authors', you have to keep them separate. Your reader will assume every sentence is yours unless you signal otherwise. Any sentence that summarizes, paraphrases, or quotes an author needs that author's name in it — either as a signal phrase or a parenthetical citation.

  • Signal phrase, usually up front: According to Turkle…
  • Parenthetical, always at the end: (Turkle).

Writing in Your Own Voice

  • Use "I" to connect the argument to what you've actually seen and lived.
  • Stay curious rather than formal. You don't need to sound academic, but you should sound like you're thinking.
  • Mean it. Argue the position you actually hold.

Format

  • MLA style
  • Minimum 1,200 words
  • In-text citations for every borrowed idea
  • Works Cited page

Drafting Sequence

  1. Outline (WA7) — thesis, three supporting reasons, and which reading supports each
  2. Rough draft (minimum 1,100 words)
  3. Peer reading — read two classmates' drafts and respond using YEPs / NOPEs / WOWs
  4. Self-assessment (WA8) — reread the assignment and your own draft
  5. Final draft
  6. Optional revision, with a written account of what you changed and why

Units — Track C: Rhetoric & AI Ethics

The class broken into units. Each unit is a major assignment plus the readings and activities that feed it. This is the same material as the schedule, cut a different way — by assignment rather than by week.

Readings named by role — “your group’s article,” “the analysis article” — are chosen by the instructor. See Readings for what each slot needs and what currently fills it.

Unit 1 — Project 1: Group Presentation on an Issue in AI Ethics

Students choose a topic strand, read its article rhetorically, and teach the class about it. Roughly Weeks 2–7.

Readings

  • The GenAI foundations reading — CORE
  • The topic-strand article for the student’s group — CORE
  • Reading: 3.1 What is Rhetoric? — CORE
  • Reading: 3.2 What is the Rhetorical Situation? — CORE
  • Reading: 2.3 How to Read Rhetorically — CORE
  • Reading: 6.1 What are Multimodal Texts? — CORE
  • Reading: 6.2 Understanding Visual Rhetoric — CORE
  • At least one from Chapter 2, reading strategies — CORE

Activities

  • In-class/Discussion: Syllabus Annotation
  • In-class/Discussion: Introductions and Your Experience with GenAI
  • WA1: Reading Annotation — GenAI Foundations
  • WA2: Reflecting on an AI-Generated Image
  • Choosing Your Topic Strand
  • WA3: Rhetorical Reading of Your Topic Article
  • WA4: Summary, Thoughts, and Rhetorical Reflection
  • Group Planning Space
  • WA8: Reflecting on Issues and Group Work

Sequence

  1. Choose a strand and join a group
  2. Read and annotate the group’s article, twice — content, then rhetoric
  3. Write the summary and rhetorical reflection
  4. Build and record the group presentation, one section each
  5. Post it, watch every other group, respond
  6. Reflect on what you learned and on the collaboration

Unit 2 — Project 2: Rhetorical Analysis Essay

Students analyze one shared article and argue whether it persuades. Roughly Weeks 8–13.

Readings

  • The analysis article — CORE, read many times
  • Reading: 2.4 Responding to Texts — CORE
  • Reading: 4.5 Revising & Orders of Concern — CORE
  • One of 4.1–4.3, the writing process — CORE
  • Reading: 3.6 Understanding Essays — FLEX
  • Reading: 8.6 Editing and Proofreading; 12.2 MLA Formatting — FLEX
  • Critical GenAI literacy: at least 1–2 readings, including at least one from Chapter 7 — CORE

Activities

  • WA5: Your Evaluative Claim
  • WA6: Expanding Your Evaluative Claim
  • WA7: Revised First Draft
  • Peer Review (CORE)
  • WA9: Using a GenAI Tool for Feedback
  • WA10: Citing Your GenAI Use

Sequence

  1. Evaluative claim, one sentence, posted for peer response
  2. Claim expanded with evidence
  3. Complete first draft
  4. Peer review using the class protocol and form
  5. Revised draft
  6. GenAI feedback on organization, plus reflection
  7. Cite the GenAI use
  8. Final draft

Unit 3 — Project 3: PSA Poster

Students argue visually, using a claim from a reading they already know. Roughly Weeks 14–16.

Readings

  • Reading: 6.1 and 6.2, revisited — CORE
  • Reading: 6.3 Visual Rhetoric and Digital Writing — CORE
  • PSA support materials: what a PSA is, example PSAs, a design-tool how-to — FLEX
  • Student sample PSAs from previous terms — FLEX

Activities

  • Prompt-drafting workshop — students write, test, and peer-review a GenAI brainstorming prompt
  • WA11: Reflecting on Your PSA

Sequence

  1. Revisit visual rhetoric and multimodality
  2. Draft and test a GenAI brainstorming prompt; get peer feedback; revise it
  3. Design the poster
  4. Post it as an image where classmates can see it
  5. Submit the reflection

What Depends on What

Read this before swapping anything. These interlocks aren’t obvious from any single assignment.

  • The topic strand chosen in Unit 1 governs all three units. It sets the reading, the group, the subject of WA4, and the source of the logical claim in the PSA. Changing the strand list changes the whole semester.
  • Project 1 is collaborative and the groups come from the strand choice. Cut the strand selection and you have to replace the grouping mechanism too.
  • WA5 becomes Project 2’s thesis. The one-sentence claim is refined in WA6 and argued across the essay.
  • WA9 produces what WA10 requires. The chat transcript is the thing students cite. Cutting one breaks the other.
  • Project 3’s logos must come from an earlier assigned article. No new research is permitted, so students need to still have their Unit 1 or Unit 2 readings.
  • Visual rhetoric is taught twice, before Project 1 and again before Project 3. Both are graded partly on design.
  • Reflection is distributed, not saved for the end. WA8 closes Unit 1, WA11 closes Unit 3, and the process reflection closes Unit 2. There is no separate final reflection paper — if your section needs one, build it onto WA11.

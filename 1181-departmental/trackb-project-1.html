Project 1: The Conversation Essay

Note to instructors

This essay asks students to put their own experience alongside an author's ideas — not to report on the reading. The four moves under "What Your Essay Needs to Do" are the assignment; everything else is support. Students use the argument reading they annotated in WA1, plus the data reading.

Overview

You'll write an essay about your own digital habits that puts your experience in conversation with two of our readings. This isn't a report on what the authors said — it's an exploration of how their ideas connect to your perspective, your experiences, and your behaviors.

Choosing Your Audience

Who needs to hear your perspective on this topic?

  • Your peers — a relatable, conversational, or even provocative tone
  • Your family or elders — a tone of reflection, explanation, or respectful challenge
  • The college community — a professional but authentic voice

Your choice shapes everything: what you explain, what you assume, and how you sound.

Prompt

Reflect on your own digital habits and connect them to the ideas in the readings, working toward an answer to the course question: How are our digital lives affecting our real lives?

What Your Essay Needs to Do

  • Start with you. Open with a story, an observation, or a moment from your life that connects to the issue in the readings. Introduce the topic before you mention any author, and say why it matters. If you can state a specific opinion about the issue, that's your thesis.
  • Bring in the authors. Introduce them and their main arguments. Summarize — but also explain how their ideas land for you. Did they put words to something you've felt? Did they miss something you think matters?
  • Enter the conversation. This is the bulk of the essay.
    • Respond to the authors. How do their ideas reflect or distort your own experience? Use specific examples from the readings, and use your own life to show where they're right — or where they're not.
    • Analyze how an author wrote the piece. What choices did they make? What worked? Would their approach work for the audience you chose?
  • Answer "so what?" Close by looking forward. Based on the readings and your own life, what should your audience take away?

Using the Readings

Use two readings: the data reading, and the argument reading you annotated.

Use no more than two short quotes from each. Quotes should help you interpret your own experience, not replace it. Some ways that works:

  • A quote from the argument reading that names a tension you've noticed in your own life
  • A figure from the data reading that your experience confirms — or contradicts
  • A concept from either that helps explain something that happened to you

Whenever you quote: introduce the author and the idea in your own words, then explain why it matters to your experience. Avoid dropped quotes — quotations that appear with no introduction and no follow-up.

Use only assigned course readings. No outside sources.

Writing in Your Own Voice

  • Use "I." You're the expert on your own life.
  • Don't perform "academic." Write the way you'd explain something complicated to someone you respect.
  • Disagreement is material. If you argued with the author in your head, or a passage confused you, write about that. Some of the best insights come from the parts of a text we struggle with.

Format

  • MLA style
  • Minimum 1,000 words
  • In-text citations for every borrowed idea
  • Works Cited page

Drafting Sequence

  1. Rough draft (minimum 700 words)
  2. Peer reading — read two classmates' drafts and respond using YEPs / NOPEs / WOWs
  3. Final draft
  4. Optional revision, with a written account of what you changed and why

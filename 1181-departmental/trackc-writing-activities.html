Writing Activities (Flex) — Track C

Note to instructors

These scaffold toward the three projects. You may modify, replace, or add as needed. Each carries a note on what it’s doing and what depends on it — check those before cutting anything. Nothing here names a specific reading; see Readings for what fills each slot.

The original course runs these as weekly online work with a fixed rhythm — a reading check, a discussion, then a short write, every week. They’re written here as activities, so they work as written assignments, in-class work, or discussions depending on your section.

Introduction Activities

These two open the term and are not part of the numbered sequence.

Syllabus Annotation

First week. Students annotate the course syllabus itself, which front-loads the GenAI policy and the late-work rules while teaching the annotation tool they’ll use all semester.

  • Annotate the syllabus, marking anything you have a question about, anything that surprises you, and the policies that will most affect how you work this term.

Modality

This can be done as an in-class activity or as an online discussion board, depending on the class modality.

Introductions and Your Experience with GenAI

Builds community and surfaces what students already believe about AI before any reading tells them what to think.

Four parts:

  1. Make and share a “propaganda I’m not falling for” image. List at least three societal norms or marketing tactics you find misleading, manipulative, or problematic — at least one about AI — and overlay the list on an image of you or one that represents you.
  2. Introduce yourself — name, career goals, anything you want the class to know.
  3. Answer three questions:
    • Which writing technologies do you use, how often, and what would writing be like without them?
    • Have you used GenAI? For what — brainstorming, generating text, study help, fun? What was it like?
    • Based on the GenAI foundations reading, what are your initial thoughts about how GenAI might affect your college writing? Useful tool, potential risk, or something else?
  4. Respond to two classmates, 3–4 sentences each. If you’re stuck, use one of these:
    • Yes, and — you agree and want to add to it
    • Yes, but — you agree but want to play devil’s advocate
    • No, because — you respectfully disagree, with counter-examples or questions

You aren’t graded on how “well” you write here.

Modality

This can be done as an in-class activity or as an online discussion board, depending on the class modality.

WA1: Reading Annotation — GenAI Foundations

The first close reading, on the foundations text. Establishes the annotation habit.

  • Annotate the assigned GenAI foundations reading, marking the author’s main claims, anything that surprises you, and anything you disagree with or don’t follow.

WA2: Reflecting on an AI-Generated Image

First analytical writing of the term, and the first time students apply visual rhetoric — to a case where an AI system’s output carries an argument nobody intended.

  • Look at an AI-generated image supplied by your instructor, along with the prompt that produced it and any published analysis of the pattern it illustrates.
  • Write a 150–200 word response:
    1. React. Is this what you expected the tool to produce? Why or why not?
    2. Analyze the representation. How does the image portray its subject? What do you notice? What’s missing?
    3. Reflect. Does the output reinforce, challenge, or complicate assumptions about gender, race, or professional identity?

WA3: Rhetorical Reading of Your Topic Article

The heart of Unit 1. Two passes over the same article — one for content, one for rhetoric. Everything in Project 1 comes out of this.

Read and annotate your group’s article twice.

First pass — comprehension and summary. Annotate for:

  1. What ethical issue is being explored?
  2. What reasons does the author give for why this is a concern?
  3. What examples, stories, or anecdotes does the author use?
  4. Which reasons and examples are most convincing, and why?
  5. Does the author have an opinion on the issue? What is it?

Second pass — rhetorical situation. Reading rhetorically means going past what a text says to how and why it says it. Identify:

  • Author — what experience or authority do they have on this subject? What values do they hold? How invested are they in the topic?
  • Audience — who is being addressed? Look at where it was published and what you can learn about that publication’s readers. What are their backgrounds, values, and interests? How open are they to this author?
  • Context — what prompted this text at this moment? Was there a debate, and what were the positions in it?
  • Purpose — is the author trying to inform, convince, define, announce, or activate? What do they want the audience to do?

WA4: Summary, Thoughts, and Rhetorical Reflection

Turns the annotation into prose and rehearses the Project 1 presentation content.

Using your annotations, write 200–250 words covering:

  1. Summary — What is the article about? What ethical issue does it focus on? What’s the author’s main point, and what reasons support it?
  2. Your thoughts — What did you think before reading? How did the article affect your thinking? Did your perspective change?
  3. Rhetorical reflection — What did you learn about the author’s background and credentials on this topic? What’s their purpose? What do you know about their audience, and how do you know? How did thinking about author, audience, purpose, and context change how you understand the argument? Are you more or less convinced after reading rhetorically?

Be specific — refer to particular points the author makes. Attribute the article clearly.

WA5: Your Evaluative Claim

One sentence. This becomes Project 2’s thesis, so the constraint is the point.

  • Write one sentence only making an evaluative claim about how persuasive the analysis article is.
  • Your claim should foreshadow your reasoning — hinting at how the author’s evidence, appeals, or handling of audience and purpose strengthens or weakens the argument.
  • Respond to peers’ claims; use their feedback to refine yours.

WA6: Expanding Your Evaluative Claim

Gathers the evidence the essay will run on.

  • Refine your claim based on peer feedback, and state it in one clear sentence.
  • Support it with at least one piece of evidence from the article — a short quotation or paraphrase showing the author using ethos, pathos, or logos.
  • For each example, explain in 2–3 sentences how that choice affects the audience or contributes to the article’s persuasiveness.
  • Close with 3–5 sentences on what you’re noticing about the author’s approach and how this evidence might fit your essay.

WA7: Revised First Draft

The draft-to-revision hinge.

  1. Re-read the Project 2 description and the sample essay.
  2. Using your outline, your earlier short writes, and your annotations, compose a complete first draft.
  3. Post it for peer review.
  4. Read your peer feedback and the revision reading, then revise.
  5. Submit the revised draft.

WA8: Reflecting on Issues and Group Work

Closes Unit 1. Two jobs at once — content reflection and collaboration reflection.

Write 300–500 words in paragraphs, responding to:

  • What did you learn from your peers’ presentations?
  • Did your position on the issue change after seeing them? How, or why not?
  • What did you learn about rhetoric and visual rhetoric in this unit, and how did it apply to your own presentation and the ones you watched?
  • What was productive or challenging about working with your group?

WA9: Using a GenAI Tool for Feedback

The centerpiece of the GenAI-as-tool half. Students get a prescribed prompt so the exercise is comparable across the class and so they see what a well-constrained prompt looks like.

  • Using the prompt your instructor supplies, ask a GenAI chat tool for feedback on your draft’s organization. A good prompt for this asks for a specific number of concrete observations, forbids the tool from rewriting your text, and explicitly tells it not to flatter, over-explain, or agree automatically.
  • Participate actively. Redirect the tool if it drifts. You may ask it to demonstrate what it means, but you may not copy anything it writes into your own draft. Instead, revise a passage yourself, paste your revision back, and ask for feedback on that.
  • Save and share the conversation.
  • Write a 500–750 word reflection: How helpful was it? Did it tell you anything you didn’t know? How will you use what it said? What were its limits? What did you gain, and what might you have lost? What will you do differently next time?

Be specific — cite moments from your own conversation. Vague reflections don’t earn credit. The reflection may not be written with an AI tool.

Offer an alternative assignment to any student uncomfortable using these tools.

WA10: Citing Your GenAI Use

Small and concrete. Makes the transparency abstract from WA9 into an actual citation.

  • Using MLA guidance for citing generative AI, write a short note explaining your use of the tool and cite it. You’ll need: a description of what was generated (including the prompt), the name of the tool, its version, its publisher, the date, and the URL of your conversation.

WA11: Reflecting on Your PSA

Closes Unit 3 and the semester.

Write 350–500 words covering:

  1. Rhetorical situation and audience — who is your audience, and why did you choose the tone, language, and visuals you did for them?
  2. Ethos, pathos, logos — how does your PSA use each? For logos, name the article, paraphrase the claim you used, and say where it appears on the poster.
  3. Visual rhetoric — explain two concrete design choices and how each supports persuasion for this audience.
  4. GenAI transparency — if you used a GenAI tool at any point, cite it and explain in 2–3 sentences what you kept, what you removed, and why. If you didn’t, say so.
  5. Final evaluation — one sentence on the poster’s overall effectiveness and one next step to strengthen it.

Unnumbered / Standalone

Choosing Your Topic Strand

Early, and consequential. This one choice determines the student’s reading, their presentation group, and the source of their PSA’s logical claim.

  • Skim the available topic strands and their articles, and pick the one that interests you.
  • Sign up in the shared thread so groups can form.
  • Introduce yourself to your group and say why you chose this issue.

Group Planning Space

An ungraded working area where each group coordinates who takes which section, agrees on slide design, and sets internal deadlines.

Peer Review (CORE)

Note to instructors

This is a core assignment, not a flex activity. The original uses a named protocol (CARES) with a required feedback form, and the form is what makes the feedback specific — students who don’t use it don’t get credit. Whatever protocol you use, give students a structured form rather than open comments.

  • Post a complete first draft of Project 2 by the midweek deadline.
  • Claim a peer’s paper by replying to it, and don’t claim one that’s already claimed — that way everyone gets a review. Don’t claim a paper you aren’t going to review.
  • Learn the class feedback protocol before you start.
  • Read your peer’s paper and give feedback using the class form, typed.
  • Post the completed form in response to your peer’s draft.

The original accepts no late work on either half of peer review, on the reasoning that a late review leaves a classmate with nothing to revise from.
